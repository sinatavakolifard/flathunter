Know your rights as a tenant

German law is designed to protect your right to housing on fair terms. In a competitive market some landlords push against those limits, and occasionally past them. These are the things worth checking before you sign anything.

The Mietspiegel

The Mietspiegel is the reference table of typical rents for comparable apartments — by size, age, condition and neighbourhood. It is the legally recognised basis for judging whether a rent is reasonable, and for contesting increases in an existing tenancy.

For Düsseldorf it is compiled by the Mieterverein together with Haus und Grund, and is available to download free of charge. Look up the figure for the flat you are considering before you agree a price.

Mietwucher — excessive rent

There are limits on how far a rent may exceed the local comparable rate. Charging around 20% above it can already expose a landlord to a fine, and at roughly 50% above it becomes a criminal offence. If the asking rent looks far out of line with the Mietspiegel, that is worth knowing before you commit, not after.

Unfair contract terms

A fair price does not guarantee a fair contract. Ask for a draft as early as you can and read it against the rental provisions of the BGB, which set out the baseline rules that apply whatever the contract says. Clauses that look unusual frequently are — for instance obligations to renovate on a fixed schedule, or blanket bans on subletting, are often unenforceable.

Get advice before you sign

If something feels wrong, a tenants' association will review a contract for you, usually for a modest annual membership. In Düsseldorf that is the Mieterverein Düsseldorf. Joining before a dispute arises is considerably more useful than joining during one.

Never transfer a deposit or a "viewing fee" before you have seen the apartment in person and signed a contract. Requests to pay in advance, especially where the landlord claims to be abroad, are the most common rental scam.
